# CV composition rules

Use these rules when composing or substantially restructuring a CV from supplied evidence. They are intentionally independent of any one scanner, ATS implementation, or tailoring protocol.

The objective is a focused, defensible, readable CV that presents the strongest relevant evidence without being constrained by the source document's original wording or layout.

## 1. Relevance comes before source ordering

The source CV is evidence, not a layout template.

You may reorder sections and entries so the strongest evidence for the target role is easiest to find.

When space is limited:

1. remove or shorten low-value and redundant material;
2. condense relevant material without erasing useful evidence;
3. reframe or decompose work to expose stronger role-relevant facets;
4. preserve high-value evidence even if it requires slightly more space.

Do not remove strong evidence merely to satisfy a page-count target.

## 2. Preserve evidence, not sentence structure

Do not assume every source bullet should survive as one final bullet.

You may:

- rewrite bullets from scratch;
- combine redundant points;
- split overloaded bullets;
- synthesize multiple supported details;
- change emphasis;
- introduce supported implementation detail;
- surface an important facet that was previously buried.

Preserve the underlying facts, attribution, dates, provenance, and defensibility.

## 3. Keep the document structurally coherent

Treat the CV as one visual and structural system.

Keep reasonably consistent:

- typography;
- heading hierarchy;
- spacing rhythm;
- alignment;
- date treatment;
- bullet conventions;
- link presentation;
- use of accents;
- entry structure.

Sections may differ when their content genuinely requires a different presentation, but they should not look like unrelated templates assembled together.

## 4. Allow unequal depth when relevance differs

Comparable entries should be easy to scan, but they do not need identical bullet counts.

Give more space to:

- stronger evidence;
- more relevant work;
- technically richer work;
- unusual or differentiating accomplishments.

Do not pad weak entries, duplicate facts, or remove useful detail merely to make sections symmetrical.

## 5. Recompose, split, and reframe work when provenance remains clear

A project, experience entry, research activity, publication, or subsystem may be reframed around the aspect most useful to the target role.

For example, the same project may reasonably be presented primarily as:

- frontend architecture;
- backend systems;
- data engineering;
- ML/retrieval;
- developer tooling;
- research;
- reliability;
- performance;
- product ownership.

Do not make one body of work appear to be several unrelated accomplishments or independent engagements.

## 6. Preserve useful supporting links

Keep repositories, papers, demos, portfolio pages, publications, documentation, and similar links when they help verify or understand important work.

Remove or alter a displayed link only for a concrete reason, such as:

- broken;
- private or inaccessible;
- unsafe;
- unrelated;
- misleading;
- unsupported;
- not reliably usable in the final document;
- impossible to retain without a real layout problem.

Generic ATS anxiety or stylistic preference is not enough by itself to remove a useful link.

## 7. Disclose location conservatively

Treat street-level location as selectively disclosed information.

As a general default:

- use a full address only when there is a concrete local reason to do so;
- otherwise prefer city plus state/province/region and country when useful;
- do not invent a residence, commute, or relocation date;
- only state willingness to relocate when the evidence or user instruction supports it.

Preserve the candidate's actual broader location.

## 8. Prefer a restrained visual default

Default to a simple, readable presentation:

- white or very light page background;
- black or near-black body text;
- restrained high-contrast accent only when useful;
- conventional hierarchy;
- readable spacing and margins.

Do not sacrifice readability, accessibility, print quality, or machine readability for visual novelty.

## 9. Prefer the simplest suitable template

Use the simplest supported template unless the content, role, application type, or user preference gives a concrete reason to choose another.

Do not change templates merely to make the CV look more distinctive.

When a non-default template materially changes the presentation, make the reason reviewable when the workflow supports review notes.

## 10. Use conventional, recognizable headings

Prefer headings such as:

- Experience;
- Professional Experience;
- Projects;
- Selected Projects;
- Education;
- Skills;
- Technical Skills;
- Research;
- Publications;
- Certifications.

Creative section naming should not make the document harder for a recruiter or parser to interpret.

Do not sacrifice semantic clarity for visual personality.

## 11. Treat page count as a target, not a hard constraint

One page is a common target for early-career and standard industry applications, but it is not a universal rule.

Additional pages can be appropriate for:

- research-heavy candidates;
- publication-heavy candidates;
- senior candidates;
- evidence-heavy technical profiles;
- cases where preserving important qualifications requires more space.

A slightly longer readable CV is better than an unreadably compressed one-page CV.

## 12. Use page space deliberately

For a one-page target, avoid both overcrowding and accidental underfilling.

If meaningful space remains, improve the page in roughly this order:

1. restore or add high-value supported evidence;
2. expand overly compressed bullets with useful technical detail;
3. surface another relevant facet of existing work;
4. add another supported entry or bullet when it improves the application;
5. only then use modest spacing adjustments to improve balance.

Do not fill space with repetition, generic soft skills, decorative noise, or inflated prose.

## 13. Readability beats compression

Do not achieve a page target through:

- unreadably small text;
- excessively tight line spacing;
- cramped margins;
- dense ambiguous layouts;
- excessive abbreviation;
- removal of strong evidence solely for space.

Edit and prioritize content before compressing the layout.

## 14. Preserve machine-readable structure

Important information should remain clear in ordinary document flow.

Avoid presentation choices that make it difficult to identify:

- employer;
- job title;
- dates;
- degree;
- institution;
- project title;
- section boundaries;
- contact information.

ATS compatibility should improve clarity and terminology, not distort the candidate's history.

## 15. Keep important terminology recognizable

Preserve standard names for:

- technologies;
- frameworks;
- qualifications;
- degrees;
- organizations;
- certifications;
- skills.

Use target-role terminology when it accurately describes the evidence, but do not keyword-stuff.

## 16. Preserve strong evidence during simplification

When only part of an entry is relevant, prefer shortening or reframing the entry instead of deleting the whole thing.

Preserve useful:

- technical details;
- outcomes;
- scope;
- implementation decisions;
- research contributions;
- responsibilities;
- quantitative evidence.

Do not replace specific evidence with vague claims merely to save space.

## 17. Make editorial trade-offs consciously

Removing previously visible evidence can be reasonable when the target role rewards a different emphasis.

Before removing a strong fact, ask:

- Is it relevant to this role?
- Is it duplicated elsewhere?
- Is stronger evidence competing for the same space?
- Can it be condensed instead of removed?
- Does removing it create a misleading picture of the candidate?

Do not automatically restore every source fact merely because it existed before.

## 18. Avoid artificial inflation

Do not:

- repeat the same accomplishment across multiple sections;
- split one accomplishment into several bullets solely to create volume;
- present closely related subsystems as unrelated projects;
- imply separate employment or research activity where none existed;
- inflate responsibility, scope, seniority, or outcomes.

Decomposition is a presentation technique, not a way to manufacture experience.

## 19. Write naturally and specifically

Apply the project's natural-writing guidance when available.

Prefer:

- concrete actions;
- technologies;
- decisions;
- constraints;
- implementation detail;
- outcomes.

Avoid generic corporate prose, formulaic keyword stuffing, and vague claims that could apply to almost any candidate.

## 20. Explain only meaningful exceptions

When the workflow supports review notes, explain significant decisions such as:

- choosing a materially different template;
- substantially restructuring a major body of work;
- omitting otherwise relevant evidence;
- materially exceeding the expected page target;
- making a deliberate evidence trade-off.

Do not create review noise for routine rewriting, ordering, or formatting changes.

## Conflict priority

When composition goals conflict, prefer:

1. explicit user instructions;
2. factual defensibility and provenance;
3. readability and accessibility;
4. preservation of strong relevant evidence;
5. natural, specific writing;
6. target-role relevance;
7. machine/ATS clarity;
8. visual polish;
9. arbitrary page-count or symmetry preferences.

A lower-priority objective should not materially compromise a higher-priority one.

## Final review test

Before accepting the composition, ask:

- Is the strongest role-relevant evidence easy to find?
- Did I preserve the substance of important source evidence even when rewriting it?
- Does the document read as one coherent system?
- Are differences in bullet depth justified by relevance rather than inconsistency?
- Are useful links and factual identifiers preserved?
- Is the page readable without artificial compression?
- Did I make intentional trade-offs rather than blindly preserve the source layout?
- Did any restructuring accidentally inflate the candidate's history?
- Could a recruiter and a parser identify the major sections and entries easily?

If not, revise the composition rather than merely polishing the wording.
